Design summary

Objectives

A feature counts as seen when changing it (boundary distance ±25%, kv/kh ×2, ω ×2, λ ×3 and so on) moves the derivative by at least 10% and the pressure by at least five gauge-resolution steps. The buildup should run about 1.5 times past that point. For a DST the recommended main flow is at least 4 hours and the main buildup at least 1.5 times the main flow.

Deconvolution

Turns the whole test (every flow and shut-in period) into one equivalent constant-rate drawdown as long as the test itself, so features beyond the longest single buildup can be seen. Uses regularized deconvolution (von Schroeter–Levitan): the log-derivative of the unit-rate response is solved on a log-time grid, with initial pressure and, optionally, rates as unknowns.

How this works

A pressure transient test changes the rate at the well and records how bottomhole pressure responds. The shape of that response, read on a log-log plot of pressure change and its derivative, tells you what the reservoir around the well looks like. This tool turns gauge data into that diagnostic, fits reservoir models to it, and checks the result against other evidence.

The log-log diagnostic

Two curves are plotted against elapsed time Δt on log-log axes: the pressure change Δp since the rate change, and the Bourdet derivative Δp′ = dΔp / d ln(Δte). The derivative is the workhorse: each flow regime gives it a characteristic slope, and it is flat whenever flow is radial.

Concepts behind the tool

Superposition and rate history

A buildup is not a clean step: every rate change before it is still propagating. The tool adds the response of each earlier rate step (superposition) and computes the derivative against superposition time, which removes most of the history effect. That is why the rate table on Data & QC matters: wrong durations or rates bend the late-time derivative and can look like a boundary.

Derivative smoothing L

The Bourdet derivative takes slopes between points at least L log cycles apart. Larger L hides noise but also flattens real features; 0.1 to 0.2 is typical. If a feature disappears when L changes, it was noise.

Models

A model is a well type (vertical, fractured, limited entry) × reservoir (homogeneous, dual porosity, radial composite) × boundary (infinite, fault, intersecting faults, channel, closed rectangle). Solutions are computed in Laplace space and inverted numerically (Stehfest); boundaries use image wells. Auto match adjusts the unlocked parameters to minimise the log misfit of Δp and Δp′ inside the fit window.

Non-uniqueness

Several models can fit one buildup equally well. A rising derivative may be a fault, a composite zone or a rate-history error. The Compare tab, the multi-period overlay, geology, and seismic decide between them, not the fit error alone.

Multi-period overlay

On Interpretation, Overlay other periods plots every other flow and shut-in period of the record, scaled to the interpreted period's rate change, together with the same model run through each period's own rate history. One model has to explain all of them. A model that fits the final buildup but misses the flow periods signals a rate error, changing skin during clean-up, or phase effects.

Gas wells

Gas properties vary with pressure, so pressure is replaced by normalized pseudo-pressure. Results still read in psi and the oil equations apply.

Phase redistribution, limited entry and coning

After shut-in, gas or water segregating in the wellbore can raise or lower the gauge pressure. This makes a hump or dip that is not reservoir behaviour; the phase-redistribution option models it and plots a corrected derivative. With a limited-entry completion near a gas cap or aquifer, the contacts give spherical flow then pressure support. The coning check compares critical rate and breakthrough time with the GOR and water cut you measured.

r-z simulator

A finite-volume black-oil model on a radial-vertical grid around the well. It handles what analytical models cannot: gas and water moving to the perforations, gas coming out of solution below bubble point, and layered permeability. The saturation section shows how fluids move during the test. Calibrate adjusts k, kv/kh and skin until the simulated pressure, and optionally GOR and water cut, match the gauge.

IPR and FMB

IPR fits stabilised rate–pressure pairs to give productivity index or deliverability and open-flow potential. Flowing material balance uses rate-normalised pressure against cumulative production during boundary-dominated flow; its intercept gives connected in-place volume.

Gauge data editing

On Data & QC, the selection tools remove data without changing the raw record. Range masks a time window. Lasso omits every point you draw around; hold Alt (Option) while drawing to restore points instead. Click toggles one point. Undo steps back, and Restore all clears every mask and omission. Points omitted in the interpreted period are also left out of deconvolution.

Deconvolution

Every rate change in a test is a step in the same underlying system. If that system is linear, the measured pressure is the convolution of the rate history with one unit-rate response. Deconvolution inverts that relationship to recover the response, as if the well had flowed at constant rate for the whole test. A DST with a 12-hour buildup inside a 30-hour test can then show the reservoir out to 30 hours of flow, about 1.6 times further in radius.

How it is solved here

The unknown is z(σ) = ln(dΔpu/d ln t), the log of the unit-rate derivative, on a grid in σ = ln t (von Schroeter, Horne and Gringarten; Levitan). Working in the log of the derivative keeps the response positive and makes it smooth. Levenberg–Marquardt minimizes

E = Σ ρ( (pmeas − pi + Σk Δqk U(t − tk)) / σ ) + λ Σ (curvature of z)² + Σ (rate corrections / 10%)²

ρ is a Huber weight, so a few bad samples (often the first seconds of a period, where storage changes faster than the gauge samples) cannot steer the late response. The first term fits the measured pressures. λ (Smoothing) trades fit against smoothness. Initial pressure pi is estimated by default. Rate correction adjusts each flow rate except the reference one, and is held near 1 by the last term.

When to trust it

The suitability panel checks the test before and after the run, and gives a verdict:

  • Linearity: fails when gas or water can reach the perforations (gas cap or aquifer in the model), when GOR or water cut changes between flow periods, or when pressure falls below the bubble point. Mobility then changes with rate and time, so no single response exists, and deconvolution will invent one, often with a false boundary.
  • Rates: warns if rates were typed rather than measured, or vary within a flow period. Deconvolution trusts rates exactly unless rate correction is on.
  • Initial pressure: without an initial shut-in, pi trades off against late-time boundary features.
  • Wellbore effects: phase redistribution and changing skin during clean-up differ between periods. Untick or omit the affected data.
  • Data: gauge noise against the derivative level, and how much longer the test is than its longest period (the potential gain).
  • After the run: the reconstruction must match every period to within noise. An estimated pi far from the measured one, or rate corrections over 15%, point to inconsistent data.
  • Consistency: each buildup is deconvolved on its own, using the full rate history and the fitted pi. If they disagree, the periods do not share one response, and the combined result is a compromise rather than the reservoir.

The shaded band on the deconvolved plot is time beyond the longest single period, and the last half log cycle is the least constrained. Treat a feature that appears only there as a hypothesis to test, for example against seismic or a longer production period.
